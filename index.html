Abstract

This article is a deep dive into the function of several of the components of a tokamak nuclear fusion reactor which are essential for the process to take place. This includes the blanket region, the neutron beam injection systems, magnetic plasma control technologies and cryostat systems. It also covers some of the problems associated with these components, and how researchers have designed the specialised systems to overcome these issues covered in a concise but straightforward way

Introduction

Nuclear fusion has begun to establish itself as a safe, economical, and unlimited energy source (Reimerdes, et. al, 2022) in the modern world. The purpose of this article is to focus on Tokamak fusion reactors, several of the technologies that make the tokamak fusion process possible in the first place, as well as some of the problems faced in current research on tokamak fusion. Designs of tokamaks are highly variable, but put simply, a tokamak is a toroidal (roughly donut) shaped mechanism (T. G. Brown, June 2018) designed to superheat plasma in a vacuum to heat up a lithium ‘blanket’, evaporating water (more on this later), which turns a turbine to produce energy.

Analysis and discussion

The Blanket Region
In a conventional fusion reactor plant, the steam drives a turbine, resulting in the generation of electric power (IAEA, 2023) The method used to heat up the coolant in a reactor is via its ‘blanket’ region consisting of the first wall, the breeding blanket-and-shield, and a vacuum chamber. “Each of these components is assumed to be a toroidally homogeneous, monolithic structure” (J. P. Freidberg, F. J. Mangiarotti, J. Minervini, July 2015) so it is a uniform shape throughout. The purpose of the blanket region is to carry out multiple processes:
First wall
- The exposed part of the inside of the core.
- Is made of high-melting-point armour materials like tungsten and (less commonly) beryllium to survive the intense heat fluctuations and particle erosion of the contained plasma [5].
Breeding blanket
- Fusion reactors run on the hydrogen isotopes deuterium and tritium. Deuterium is easy to obtain but natural tritium “is extremely rare in nature” [6] and has a short 12.3-year half-life, so it is scarce. To produce the tritium fuel, high-energy fusion neutrons collide with “lithium-containing material to breed tritium and shield the magnets and other components from outgoing neutrons” [6].
Divertor
- The part of the blanket that absorbs the kinetic energy of the neutrons and converts it into high-temperature heat which is transferred away by the coolants the drive the electricity-generating turbines [5][7].
Radiation Shield Blanket
- Made from high-grade stainless steel. They act as a heavy barrier to protect the vacuum vessel walls and the extremely sensitive superconductor magnets from neutron bombardment and heat stress [8][5]
Magnetic Confinement
A tokamak uses electromagnets to curve field lines into a closed, donut-shaped loop called a torus to keep the superheated plasma contained [12]. Because the magnetic coils are physically clustered closer together near the inner wall, the magnetic field is inherently stronger on the inside than the outside [13], causing electrons to drift downward and ions to drift upwards.
The solution to this is a three-dimensional twisted magnetic field (called a poloidal field) [12]. By forcing the magnetic field lines to twist into a helix, a single particle tracking along a field line continuously samples both the top and bottom regions of the reactor, averaging out the charge separation, maintaining an overall consistent shape of the plasma flow [14].
Tokamaks also use ‘Shaping Coils’. The function of these is to create complex 3D magnetic fields to hold the plasma away from the reactor walls [15], reducing heat loss and turbulence [16].

Neutron beam Injection
One of the main challenges faced in tokamak fusion is the fact that normally fusion can take place at temperatures of around 15 million degrees Celsius [10] due to the massive gravitational force of stars exerting extra pressure to carry out fusion.
On Earth, our gravity is much lower, so fusion reactors must produce immense temperatures of up to 150 million ˚C to compensate for the reduced atmospheric pressure [9]. Neutron beam Injection Is one of the processes used to heat up the fuel to initiate the fusion reaction. How it works:

1. Ion extraction – positive ions are extracted from an RF plasma source, which uses high-frequency electromagnetic waves to ionize gases into plasma [11]. 2. The positive ions are accelerated through a three-grid system with 774 apertures each.[11] 3. The accelerated positive ions pass through a gas-filled neutralizer tube where they undergo charge exchange with neutral D_2, converting them into neutral atoms [11] 4. Remaining non-neutralized ions are deflected by a bending magnet into heavily water-cooled ion dumps, while the neutral beam continues straight [11]. 5. The neutral beam travels through the beam duct to the torus [11]. 6. beams inject tangentially into the tokamak plasma for fusion drive and current drive [11].
